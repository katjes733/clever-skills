## 1. Core facts

- **Election Day: Tuesday, November 3, 2026.** All 435 U.S. House seats, 35 U.S. Senate seats (33 regular Class II seats plus 2 special elections), and 36 governorships are up. Also on many ballots: attorneys general and other statewide offices, state legislatures, county and city offices, school boards, judges, and ballot measures.
- The president is **not** on the ballot. Voters decide Congress, governors, and state and local government.
- Primaries for U.S. House seats ran from March 3 through September 15, 2026. Most voters are now choosing among general-election nominees. Some states and offices have later runoffs (section 7).
- Primary rules (open, closed, top-two) are mostly irrelevant for the general election, but matter for runoffs and for any special or later contests.

## 2. What is on a typical midterm ballot

Federal: U.S. House (one seat for the voter's district), U.S. Senate (in states with a seat up, including 2 special elections). Statewide: governor and other executive offices in many states, attorney general, secretary of state, treasurer or comptroller, superintendent of education, judges and retention votes, ballot measures. District and local: state senate and house, county board, sheriff, district attorney, assessor, clerk, municipal offices, school board, special districts. Use the voter's official sample ballot and county page for the exact list.

## 3. Rules and rulings that changed the landscape

- **Watson v. Republican National Committee (decided June 29, 2026, 5 to 4; docket 24-1260, per the Alaska Division of Elections and PolitiFact).** The Supreme Court held that federal Election Day statutes do not stop a state from counting mail ballots that are postmarked by Election Day but arrive afterward, if state law allows it. It did not require states to do so. PolitiFact listed 14 states with such grace periods (Alaska, California, Illinois, Maryland, Massachusetts, Mississippi, Nevada, New Jersey, New York, Oregon, Texas, Virginia, Washington, West Virginia); other outlets count D.C. as well, and windows differ (for example 3 to 14 days) and may have changed recently. Treat that list as a lead, not as your state's rule. **Practical takeaway: look up your state's receipt deadline, return mail ballots early, and use a drop box or in-person return where available.**
- **Louisiana v. Callais (decided April 29, 2026, 6 to 3, per multiple outlets and the Court's reported opinion).** The Court struck down Louisiana's second majority-Black congressional district and substantially narrowed how Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act can be used in redistricting challenges. It prompted several states to redraw maps (section 4).
- **SAVE America Act (federal proof of citizenship and photo ID for federal elections).** Passed the House on February 11, 2026, and had not passed the Senate as of reporting in August 2026. As of this snapshot, no federal documentary-proof-of-citizenship requirement was found to be in effect. Several states passed their own versions in 2025 and 2026 (reporting counted seven). A federal court permanently enjoined parts of the March 2025 executive order on election procedures as of June 2026 (per a secondary source; verify). **Check your state's rules directly; do not assume a federal requirement applies.**
- **Voter ID and citizenship measures.** Five state constitutional measures on voter ID appear on the November 3 ballot (Arizona, California, Nevada, North Carolina, Ohio; Oklahoma's was earlier). A measure passed in November generally does not change the rules for this election. Check effective dates.

## 4. Redistricting: why your district number may differ from 2024

Mid-decade redistricting is unusual. Per a single aggregator (PolicyMap, updated September 28, 2026, which counts against Loyola Law School's All About Redistricting), **ten states** that adopted new congressional maps outside the normal post-census cycle since mid-2025, with **eight in force for November 2026**: Texas, North Carolina, Ohio, California, Utah, Florida, Tennessee, and Louisiana. Litigation is live in several; the tracker says Ohio's faces no live challenge. Other trackers count differently (NCSL and Ballotpedia also arrive at ten, but include Alabama and exclude Virginia), and rulings can change within days. Key exceptions, per the same tracker:

- **Missouri:** a new 2025 map is **not** in use for November. The Supreme Court (September 25, 2026) left the 2022 map in place for this election, and the 2025 map goes to voters as **Proposition A** on the November 3 ballot (Yes approves the 2025 map; No keeps the 2022 lines).
- **Virginia:** the amendment enabling a new map was struck down on May 8, 2026, so the 2021 court-drawn map stays.
- **Alabama:** votes under the congressional map its legislature passed in 2023 after a Supreme Court stay on June 2, 2026, which differs from the lines used in 2024.
- **Other items on the November ballot:** Colorado Amendment 86 (requires commission and Supreme Court approval for mid-decade congressional maps; needs 55%) and Maryland Question 3 (constitutional amendment about congressional districting standards; targets 2028).
- Mississippi held hearings about possible redistricting; no map adopted as of the tracker.

**What to do:** never reuse a 2024 district number. Have the voter confirm their current congressional district with an official lookup (state election office or the U.S. House member finder), and compare with their sample ballot. A ZIP code may span more than one district.

Military and overseas voters have separate deadlines and ballot options (FVAP.gov).

## 6. Ballot measures

- As of September 15, 2026, Ballotpedia counted **164 statewide measures** certified in 40 states for 2026: **145 on the November ballot** and 19 decided earlier. Litigation can still remove or invalidate a measure.
- Common themes: voting requirements and citizenship, abortion, marijuana and psychedelics, sex and gender policy, housing (six statewide housing measures in California, Hawaii, Massachusetts, and Rhode Island), term limits and initiative rules, taxes and property tax limits, redistricting (Missouri Prop A, Colorado Amendment 86, Maryland Question 3). Virginia also has multiple constitutional amendments on the ballot.
- Read each measure's official text or voter pamphlet. Do not rely on its title; ballot titles can be misleading. Report who funds the Yes and No sides.

## 7. Runoffs and later rounds

- **Georgia:** general-election runoff on **December 1, 2026** if no candidate wins a majority.
- **Louisiana:** closed party primaries for congressional races for the first time in 2026 (primary May 16, runoff June 27 per earlier calendar); one source lists a **December 12, 2026** general-election runoff for U.S. House. Calendars were disrupted after *Callais*. **Verify with the Louisiana Secretary of State.**
- Other states may have special elections or runoffs. Ask the voter whether their ballot includes a later round and add it to the guide's timeline.

## 9. Interview adaptations

- Lead the issue list with federal topics when the ballot is federal-heavy: economy and costs, healthcare, immigration, democracy and election administration, climate, foreign policy, reproductive rights, gun policy, fiscal responsibility.
- For state and local, add: housing and zoning, schools and bonds, public safety, water and infrastructure, property taxes.
- Ask early whether the voter's state has a Senate race, a governor's race, or notable ballot measures, since these shape which flashpoints to research.
- Remind the voter that midterm outcomes depend on the offices on their specific ballot; the president is not one of them.
- For redistricting measures (Missouri Prop A, Colorado Amendment 86, Maryland Question 3), explain neutrally what Yes and No change and who is on each side; these are high-salience and often framed partisanly.

## 10. What was not verified

- State-by-state mail-ballot receipt rules, ID rules, and registration deadlines for all 50 states; only examples above were checked.
- Candidate lists for any specific race. Those must be researched from the voter's own ballot.
- The current status of pending redistricting cases beyond the tracker's September 28 update.
- Whether any federal election law changed after mid-August 2026.
- The exact Louisiana House calendar after *Callais*.
