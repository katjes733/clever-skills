# Election Systems Quick Guide

Explain the system the voter actually faces before asking them to decide. Rules change, so treat everything here as orientation and verify with the jurisdiction's election authority in the current session.

## 1. Voting methods

- **Plurality (first past the post).** One vote; the most votes wins even without a majority. Common in U.S. general elections, the UK House of Commons, Canada's House of Commons.
- **Two-round (runoff).** If no one gets a majority, the top two (or more) face a second round later. Used in some U.S. states (Georgia's general-election runoff, for example) and in many countries.
- **Ranked-choice / instant-runoff.** The voter ranks candidates; last-place candidates are eliminated and their ballots transfer until someone has a majority. Used in some U.S. states and cities and in Australia's House.
- **Single transferable vote (STV).** Multi-seat ranked ballots, used in Australia's Senate and Ireland.
- **Party-list proportional representation.** Voters choose a party (or a list); seats are allocated by share of votes. Used across much of Europe.
- **Mixed-member systems.** Two votes: one for a local candidate, one for a party list. Germany's Bundestag is the best-known case (first vote local, second vote party; the second vote largely decides party strength). Verify current rules.
- **Top-two / "jungle" primaries.** All candidates appear on one primary ballot regardless of party; the top two advance. Used in California and Washington; some states have used variants for specific offices.
- **Top-four with ranked-choice general (Alaska).** All candidates run in one primary; the top four advance to a ranked-choice general election. Some jurisdictions are debating changes to such systems, so verify the current rules.
- **Approval and other methods.** Appear in some localities. Verify.
- **Multi-seat "vote for N".** Several winners at once, common in school boards and councils.

## 2. Primary types (U.S.)

- **Closed.** Only registered members of a party vote in its primary.
- **Semi-closed.** Party members plus unaffiliated voters (sometimes choosing a ballot).
- **Open.** Any registered voter picks a party's ballot at the polls (some states require a public choice of ballot).
- **Nonpartisan or top-two.** One ballot for all; party labels appear but do not restrict.

Always verify: primary rules differ by state and sometimes by party and by election. If a primary has already happened, the voter is in the general or a runoff.

## 3. Country notes

### United States
- Federal elections are run by states; rules vary widely (registration deadlines, ID, mail voting, early voting, ballot receipt deadlines).
- Midterm general elections choose all House seats, about a third of the Senate, many governors, state legislators, and local officials. Presidential elections add the president.
- Verify the **ballot receipt deadline** for mail ballots in the voter's state; a 2026 Supreme Court decision confirmed that states may count postmarked ballots that arrive after Election Day if state law allows, but many states require receipt by Election Day (details in the 2026 snapshot).
- Redistricting can change district lines between elections; use current official maps.

### Canada
- Federal House of Commons: plurality in single-member ridings; the party that can command the confidence of the House forms the government. Provinces and cities have their own rules; municipal elections are provincially regulated.
- The voter chooses a local candidate; the party leader is not on the ballot (unless they run locally). Say which choice moves which outcome.

### United Kingdom
- House of Commons: plurality in single-member constituencies; the voter picks a candidate, not a Prime Minister. Photo ID is required at polling stations in Great Britain for many elections; verify per election type. Devolved parliaments (Scotland, Wales) and some local elections use different systems.

### Germany
- Bundestag: two votes. The first for a local candidate in the constituency; the second for a state party list. Recent reforms changed how seats are allocated, so confirm the current system with the Bundeswahlleiterin before explaining details. Voters also have state (Länder) and local elections with their own systems.

### Australia
- House of Representatives: ranked preferential voting; voters must number all boxes (or follow the rules for a valid vote). Senate: STV, with above-the-line and below-the-line options. Voting is compulsory for federal elections. Verify state and local rules with the relevant electoral commission.

### Elsewhere
Identify the system from the national electoral authority. Do not assume U.S. concepts (primaries, party registration, "district") apply.

## 4. How to explain a system in two sentences

- Plurality: "You vote for one candidate; whoever gets the most votes wins, even without a majority."
- Ranked choice: "You rank candidates in order of preference; if your top choice is eliminated, your vote moves to your next choice. You can rank as many as you like."
- Runoff: "If nobody wins more than half, the top two go to a second election on [date]; your vote today decides who advances."
- Mixed-member: "You have two votes: one for a local representative and one for a party. [Which one largely decides the balance of power]."
- Closed primary: "Only registered [party] voters can vote in this primary, so your registration decides which ballot you get."
